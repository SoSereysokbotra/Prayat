# ScamSim — Subscription Customer Scenarios
### Who pays, who learns, and why they keep renewing

---

## How the Subscription Model Works

ScamSim is a **paid monthly subscription**. The person who pays is not always the person who learns. There are two natural buying directions:

- **Parent → Child**: A parent subscribes and gives their child access to learn cybersecurity skills through the platform
- **Child → Parent**: An adult son or daughter subscribes and gives their elderly parent access because they are worried their parent will fall for an online scam

Both directions share the same emotional trigger: **someone they love is at risk, and they want to do something about it.**

There is also a third group — **individuals who subscribe for themselves** — typically university students or young adults who recognise the risk personally and want to build their own skills.

## Subscription Scenario 1 — Mother Subscribes for Her Teenage Daughter

**Buyer:** Sreymom, 41, runs a clothing stall in Phnom Penh  
**Learner:** Her daughter Channary, 15, uses TikTok and Telegram daily  
**Subscription direction:** Parent → Child

### The Moment She Decides to Subscribe

Sreymom overhears her daughter talking to someone on Telegram late at night. When she asks who it is, Channary says it is a friend from school — but the way she says it makes Sreymom uncomfortable. She checks the phone and sees a conversation with a profile she does not recognise, someone claiming to be a 19-year-old student offering Channary a part-time job promoting products on social media.

Sreymom does not know exactly what is happening, but something feels wrong. She asks a younger cousin who works in an office what she should do. The cousin tells her about ScamSim — a platform where young people can learn to recognise exactly these kinds of situations through realistic simulations.

### Why She Subscribes

Sreymom visits the ScamSim platform. She reads that it works like a game, it is in Khmer, and it covers the types of scams that happen specifically on Telegram and Facebook. She sees that parents can view their child's progress and scores. The monthly price is less than what she spends on a week of snacks for Channary.

She subscribes that evening. She does not frame it as punishment or a warning. She tells Channary: "This is a learning game for smart people who use the internet a lot. I want you to try it."

### What the Learner Does Inside the Platform

Channary opens it expecting to be bored. Instead she finds a simulated Telegram conversation where someone offers her a job. She makes a choice. The conversation continues. She makes another choice. By the end she has fallen for the scam inside the simulation — and the platform shows her exactly why, step by step, in Khmer she understands.

She plays three more scenarios that night without being asked.

### Why They Keep Renewing Month After Month

Sreymom sees Channary's Scam Resistance Score improving each week. New scenarios appear regularly so Channary always has something fresh to play. Channary starts mentioning things at dinner — "that's a pig butchering scam" or "you should never send money to someone you haven't met in person." Sreymom renews the subscription every month without thinking twice. It is one of the few things she has paid for that she can actually see working.

## Subscription Scenario 2 — Father Subscribes for His Son at University

**Buyer:** Piseth, 50, works as a government clerk in Kampong Cham  
**Learner:** His son Virak, 20, studies IT at a university in Phnom Penh  
**Subscription direction:** Parent → Child

### The Moment He Decides to Subscribe

Virak calls home and mentions casually that a classmate lost $200 in a crypto investment group on Telegram. Piseth does not know exactly what crypto is, but he understands that his son is now living independently in a city, managing his own money for the first time, and surrounding himself with people who are being targeted.

He asks Virak if he knows how to spot a scam. Virak says yes, but his confidence is not convincing. Piseth feels the distance of being a parent who cannot physically watch over his son anymore.

### Why He Subscribes

A colleague at his office mentions that his own daughter uses a cybersecurity learning app and that it has made her much more careful online. Piseth searches for it, finds ScamSim, and sees that one of the scenario categories is specifically about fake investment schemes and crypto fraud — exactly what Virak's classmate fell for.

He subscribes and sends the login to Virak. He tells him: "I am paying for this. Please use it. Think of it as part of your education."

### What the Learner Does Inside the Platform

Virak opens it out of respect for his father more than genuine interest. But the crypto scam scenario immediately feels familiar — the group chat, the screenshots of profits, the growing "balance" on a platform he has never heard of. He fails the simulation on his first try. He goes back and tries again. He passes on the third attempt, this time catching the withdrawal fee demand as the key red flag.

He messages his classmate the screenshot from the debrief and says: "This is exactly what happened to you."

### Why They Keep Renewing Month After Month

Piseth sees his son actually engaging with the platform through the parent dashboard. He renews each month because it costs less than the bus ticket he would take to visit Virak and check on him in person. For Piseth, the subscription is peace of mind at a distance.

## Subscription Scenario 3 — Adult Son Subscribes for His Elderly Mother

**Buyer:** Dara, 27, works as an accountant in Phnom Penh  
**Learner:** His mother Sothea, 58, lives alone in Siem Reap and uses Facebook daily  
**Subscription direction:** Adult child → Parent

### The Moment He Decides to Subscribe

Dara calls his mother on a Sunday and she mentions that someone from "the Ministry of Social Affairs" messaged her on Facebook asking her to confirm her Wing account details to receive a government subsidy. She has already replied with her phone number. She is waiting for the next message.

Dara's stomach drops. He tells her to stop replying immediately. He calls Wing's customer support. No money has been taken yet. He is lucky — this time.

He realises his mother will receive another message like this. And another. And he cannot be there to answer every call in time.

### Why He Subscribes

Dara has heard of ScamSim through a colleague. He visits the platform and sees a scenario category specifically for government impersonation scams — fake officials, fake ministries, fake subsidy schemes. The simulations are in Khmer. The interface is simple enough for someone his mother's age to navigate.

He calls his mother and explains what he is going to pay for. He walks her through how to open it on her phone. He subscribes to the family plan so both he and his mother have access.

### What the Learner Does Inside the Platform

Sothea is hesitant at first — she tells Dara it looks like a children's game. But when she plays the first scenario, a fake ministry official asking her to send personal details to receive money, she pauses. She reads the message carefully. She picks the option to ignore it. The platform tells her she made the right decision and explains why the message was fake — the Facebook page had no official verification, the request came through Messenger not an official channel, and real government programs never ask for Wing account details online.

She calls Dara afterwards and says: "I understand now. I did exactly the wrong thing last week."

### Why They Keep Renewing Month After Month

Dara renews every month without hesitation. The cost is small compared to what his mother could lose. He also uses the platform himself — there is a scenario for workplace email fraud that he finds directly relevant to his job as an accountant. He has effectively turned one subscription into protection for two people.

## Subscription Scenario 4 — Adult Daughter Subscribes for Her Father

**Buyer:** Bopha, 29, works as a nurse in Phnom Penh  
**Learner:** Her father Chantha, 61, runs a small hardware shop in Battambang  
**Subscription direction:** Adult child → Parent

### The Moment She Decides to Subscribe

Bopha's father calls her excited. Someone added him on Facebook claiming to be a businessman from Singapore looking to buy construction materials in bulk. They have been chatting for a week. The "businessman" is now asking Chantha to send a sample shipment first and promises to pay double once it arrives. He wants to know the best way to transfer money internationally.

Bopha recognises it immediately — a variant of the advance fee and goods theft scam. She tells her father not to send anything. He is disappointed and a little defensive — he says the man seemed very professional and friendly.

She hangs up feeling frightened. Not by this scam, which she stopped in time, but by the ones she will not catch because she will not know about them.

### Why She Subscribes

Bopha finds ScamSim through a health workers' Facebook group where someone shared an article about online fraud in Cambodia. She reads that ScamSim has a scenario specifically for business impersonation and fake buyer scams — exactly the type her father nearly fell for.

She subscribes for her father. She does not tell him it is because she thinks he is naive. She tells him: "I want you to play this — it is like a detective game, and I think you will be good at it." She knows her father responds well to being treated as capable rather than as someone who needs protecting.

### What the Learner Does Inside the Platform

Chantha opens it with scepticism. He plays the fake business buyer scenario and — to his surprise — he falls for it in the first round. He is genuinely shocked. He plays it again. On the second round he spots the red flags: no video call offered despite weeks of chat, payment method is unusual, no verifiable company address. He passes.

He starts playing every evening after the shop closes. He messages Bopha: "I passed five scenarios. What is my score?"

### Why They Keep Renewing Month After Month

Bopha renews every month because her father has become genuinely engaged — he sends her his scores and asks her to check if there are new scenarios. The subscription has also changed their relationship slightly. Her father now calls her to ask about suspicious messages before responding to them. The platform gave him a framework, and the habit of checking has replaced the habit of trusting too easily.

## Subscription Scenario 5 — University Student Subscribes for Themselves

**Buyer and Learner:** Vibolika, 22, studies business at a university in Phnom Penh  
**Subscription direction:** Individual → Self

### The Moment She Decides to Subscribe

Vibolika is looking for freelance work online to cover her living expenses. She has applied to three remote job listings from Telegram and one has asked her to download a file and complete a "test task." Before downloading it, she hesitates. She remembers her roommate warning her about EXE files, but she does not fully understand why they are dangerous or how to tell a legitimate task from a malicious one.

She wants to understand the rules — not just avoid one specific thing, but actually know how to protect herself across all the situations she is walking into as someone who spends hours every day on Telegram looking for income.

### Why She Subscribes

Vibolika searches online for cybersecurity courses but finds they are either in English, full of technical jargon, or built for people who already work in IT. ScamSim is different — it is in Khmer, it covers the exact situations she faces as a young person trying to earn money online, and it works like a game rather than a lecture series.

She pays for the individual subscription herself. It is her own money and her own decision. She frames it mentally as an investment in not losing money she does not have.

### What She Does Inside the Platform

She plays every scenario category available. The fake job offer scenario confirms exactly what she suspected about the Telegram listing — the request to download a file for a "test task" is a documented scam tactic. She does not download it.

She works through the crypto scenarios, the government impersonation scenarios, and the romance manipulation scenarios. She tracks her Scam Resistance Score and makes it a personal goal to reach the highest level in each category before the month ends.

### Why She Keeps Renewing Month After Month

New scenarios are added regularly, which means the platform stays relevant to new scam types that appear. She also realises the platform is useful beyond protecting herself — she starts explaining the red flags to classmates who ask how she seems to know so much about online safety. She feels knowledgeable in a way that is directly practical to her daily life, and that feeling is worth the monthly cost.

## Subscription Scenario 6 — Couple Subscribes Together

**Buyers and Learners:** Sopheak, 35, and his wife Chanmony, 33, both work in Phnom Penh  
**Subscription direction:** Household → Both adults

### The Moment They Decide to Subscribe

Sopheak receives a WhatsApp message from someone claiming to be his bank, warning him that his account is under review and he must verify his details through a link immediately. He almost clicks the link before Chanmony tells him to call the bank directly first. The bank confirms no such message was sent.

That evening they talk about how close it came. Neither of them felt confident enough to spot it on their own. They agree they should both understand this better — not just for themselves but because their parents are even more vulnerable and they are the people their parents will call when something suspicious happens.

### Why They Subscribe

They find ScamSim and subscribe to the couples or family plan. They see it as a household decision, the same way they might pay for a home security service. Both of them will use it. Both of them will be better equipped to help their parents.

### What They Do Inside the Platform

They make it a habit to each play one scenario per week and compare scores over dinner. It becomes a light competition. When Chanmony scores higher on the investment scam scenario, Sopheak goes back and replays it until he understands where he went wrong. They both complete the government impersonation scenarios and immediately share the key rules with their respective parents over the family group chat.

### Why They Keep Renewing Month After Month

The habit makes them feel more in control of something that previously felt random and threatening. New scenarios keep the content fresh. They also discover the platform has adult-specific scenarios covering workplace email fraud and fake supplier scams — directly relevant to both of their jobs. The subscription has become genuinely useful across multiple areas of their lives, not just the original concern that led them to subscribe.

## What Keeps People Subscribed

Across all six scenarios, three things drive monthly renewal:

**Visible progress.** The Scam Resistance Score gives learners and the people who pay for them something concrete to see. A parent who can see their child's score improving has a reason to keep paying. An adult child who can see their parent completing scenarios has reassurance that the subscription is working.

**New scenarios regularly.** If the content never changes, people cancel after one month. Regular new scenarios — tied to scam types that are actually appearing in Cambodia right now — give subscribers a reason to return. This is also what makes the subscription feel alive rather than static.

**Protection for more than one person.** Several of these subscribers discover the platform is useful for themselves even when they bought it for someone else. A father who subscribes for his son plays the workplace fraud scenarios. A daughter who subscribes for her father uses the fake job scenarios herself. When one subscription protects multiple people, the value is multiplied and the likelihood of cancellation drops significantly.
